================================================================
 Deadlock Prevention via Resource Ordering (Q3)
================================================================

The Problem
-----------
A deadlock requires four conditions to hold simultaneously (the Coffman
conditions): mutual exclusion, hold-and-wait, no preemption, and circular
wait. Of these, circular wait is the only one that can be eliminated purely
by policy — by controlling the *order* in which resources are requested —
without touching mutual exclusion, preemption, or hold-and-wait behavior.

In resorder_bad.c, Process A acquires Lock1 then requests Lock2, while
Process B acquires Lock2 then requests Lock1. With the deliberate delay
between acquisitions, both processes are guaranteed to be holding their
first lock when they attempt to acquire their second — producing exactly
the classic circular-wait pattern:

    Process A: holds Lock1, waits for Lock2
    Process B: holds Lock2, waits for Lock1

Neither process can proceed, and neither can release what it holds, so the
system hangs indefinitely (see resourceorder_log.txt).

The Fix: A Strict Global Order
-------------------------------
resorder_fixed.c assigns a total order to the resources: Lock1 is always
acquired before Lock2, by every process, with no exceptions. This is
sufficient to make circular wait impossible.

Proof sketch: suppose a cycle existed among processes holding and
requesting these two locks. Some process would have to hold Lock2 while
requesting Lock1. But under the global ordering rule, any process that
needs both locks must acquire Lock1 first — so a process cannot hold Lock2
without already having acquired (and still holding, or having released)
Lock1 first. It is therefore impossible for a process to hold Lock2 while
still waiting on Lock1, and the wait-for graph can never close into a
cycle.

This generalizes directly to n resources and n processes: if every process
requests resources in increasing order of a fixed global rank, no process
can ever wait on a resource "behind" one it already holds, so the
resource-allocation graph is guaranteed to remain acyclic. This is the same
principle applied at larger scale in syncdeadlock.c (Q4), with three
resource types instead of two.

Trade-off
---------
Resource ordering trades some flexibility for a hard guarantee: processes
may occasionally hold a resource longer than strictly necessary while
waiting to acquire the next one in order, even if they don't need it yet.
For this assignment's scale, that cost is negligible compared to the
correctness guarantee it buys.
